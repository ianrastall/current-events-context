## A national emergency

On 2 April 2025 President Donald Trump signed an executive order declaring that the country's trade deficits had become a national emergency.[[S001 90 FR 15041, heading and preamble]] The order found that a lack of reciprocity in trade, foreign tariffs and other barriers, and the policies of partners that held down their own wages and consumption together "constitute an unusual and extraordinary threat to the national security and economy of the United States."[[S001 Preamble, 90 FR 15041]] It pointed to a goods deficit that had grown by more than 40 per cent in five years, to $1.2 trillion in 2024.[[S001 Sec. 1, 90 FR 15044]]

{{portrait:trump}}

The remedy was a tariff on almost everything. From 5 April a duty of 10 per cent applied to goods from nearly every country, and from 9 April higher rates applied to 57 trading partners named in an annex, ranging from 11 to 50 per cent.[[S001 Sec. 3(a), 90 FR 15045; Annex I, 90 FR 15049-15050]] China's rate was 34 per cent, the European Union's 20, Japan's 24, India's 26 and Vietnam's 46; the highest, 50 per cent, fell on Lesotho.[[S001 Annex I, 90 FR 15049-15050]] Cargo already at sea was spared. So were whole categories: steel, aluminium and cars, already covered by other tariffs, and a long annex of products from copper and pharmaceuticals to semiconductors and energy.[[S001 Sec. 3(a)-(b), 90 FR 15045-15046]]

The order was the centrepiece of the year's trade policy, but not its beginning. By April the administration had already put tariffs on Canada, Mexico, China and steel, and had announced them on cars.[[S002 Sec. 2 | S004 Sec. 2(a) | S005 Sec. 2(a) | S040 Clauses 1-2 | S044 Clause 1]] In the months that followed it extended them to copper, timber and heavy trucks, and turned them on Brazil and India for reasons that had nothing to do with trade balances.[[S042 Clause 2 | S043 Clauses 1-3 | S045 Clause 1 | S031 Sec. 1-2 | S018 Sec. 2]]

This chapter argues that in 2025 the tariff stopped being an instrument of trade policy and became an all-purpose tool of presidential power. It raised a great deal of money: customs receipts more than tripled, to about $264.0 billion for the calendar year.[[S021 Customs Duties, monthly net receipts, 2024-2025]] It moved trade from one supplier to another. It did not shrink the goods deficit it had been declared to cure, which grew to $1,240.9 billion.[[S019 Annual Summary for 2025]] And its legal foundation was rejected by the trade court, the appeals court and the Supreme Court, though only after the year's duties had been collected.[[S012 Conclusion | S013 Part VI | S003 Syllabus, pp. 1-2]]

## Two kinds of power

The Constitution gives the power to lay duties to Congress, not the President, and in the litigation that followed the government conceded as much: it claimed no inherent presidential authority to impose tariffs in peacetime.[[S003 Syllabus, p. 2]] The tariffs of 2025 therefore rested on statutes in which Congress had delegated power, and the administration used two very different kinds.

The first was national security. Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 lets the President adjust imports that threaten to impair national security, after an investigation by the Commerce Department. The steel proclamation of 10 February used it to restore a 25 per cent tariff on steel from every country from 12 March, ending the exemptions that several partners had negotiated since 2018; aluminium was raised to 25 per cent the same day.[[S040 Clauses 1-2, Proclamation 10896]] The proclamation cited the Commerce Secretary's view that the earlier tariff had worked, lifting domestic steel capacity utilization above 80 per cent.[[S040 Clause 4, Proclamation 10896]] The same authority would later cover cars, copper, timber and trucks.[[S044 Clause 1 | S042 Clause 2 | S043 Clauses 1-3 | S045 Clause 1]]

The second kind of power was new. The International Emergency Economic Powers Act, known as IEEPA, allows the President to "regulate" imports in response to a declared national emergency. As the Chief Justice later observed for three members of the Supreme Court, "in IEEPA's half century of existence, no President has invoked the statute to impose any tariffs."[[S003 Syllabus, p. 3]] In 2025 it became the legal basis for the broadest tariffs of the year.

The emergencies were of several kinds. The first three orders, signed on 1 February, cited drugs: illicit drugs crossing the northern border from Canada, drugs and illegal migration across the southern border from Mexico, and the supply chain for synthetic opioids in China.[[S002 Sec. 1-2 | S004 Sec. 2(a) | S005 Sec. 2(a)]] The April order cited the trade deficit itself and the decline of manufacturing.[[S001 Preamble; Sec. 1]] Later orders cited a partner's purchases of Russian oil and, in Brazil's case, the actions of its courts.[[S018 Sec. 2 | S031 Sec. 1]] The orders reserved the power to raise rates if a partner retaliated, and the April order the power to lower them if a partner "align[ed]" with the United States on economic and national security matters.[[S002 Sec. 2(d) | S001 Sec. 4(b)-(c)]] The tariff had become a lever that could be pulled for almost any purpose.

## Neighbours first

The first targets were the closest partners. On 1 February orders imposed an additional duty of 25 per cent on goods from Canada and Mexico and 10 per cent on Canadian energy, to take effect on 4 February.[[S002 Sec. 2 | S004 Sec. 2(a)]] The same orders withdrew duty-free treatment for low-value parcels, the so-called de minimis rule.[[S002 Sec. 2(h)]]

Canada answered the same day. Its government announced counter-tariffs of 25 per cent on C$155 billion of American goods, beginning with C$30 billion of products such as orange juice, peanut butter, wine, spirits, beer and appliances, and following with C$125 billion after a three-week consultation.[[S025 News release, 1 February 2025]]

Mexico chose a different course. On 3 February President Claudia Sheinbaum announced a one-month pause in exchange for reinforcing the northern border with 10,000 members of the National Guard to stop the smuggling of drugs, and especially fentanyl.[[S027 Communiqué, 3 February 2025]] The United States, she said, would for its part work against the trafficking of arms into Mexico, and the two governments set up working groups on security and trade. Mexico announced no counter-tariffs.[[S027 Communiqué, 3 February 2025]] Canada obtained its own pause the same day, and both were formalised in orders dated 3 February.[[S001 Sec. 3(d)]]

{{portrait:sheinbaum}}

The pauses did not last. When they ended in early March, Canada's first counter-tariffs, on C$30 billion of American goods, took effect on 4 March.[[S026 Archived response page]] In early March the United States exempted goods that qualified under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement and cut the rate on Canadian potash to 10 per cent.[[S016 Preamble | S001 Sec. 3(d)]] President Sheinbaum announced that Mexican goods within the agreement would pay nothing.[[S028 Communiqué, 6 March 2025]] When the global steel and aluminium tariffs took effect on 12 March, Canada added counter-tariffs on C$29.8 billion of steel, aluminium and other goods the next day; when American tariffs on cars arrived in April, it taxed American vehicles from 9 April.[[S040 Clauses 1-2 | S026 Archived response page]]

On 2 April, the day of the global order, the Senate voted 51 to 48 to terminate the emergency on which the Canadian tariffs rested, with four Republicans, Susan Collins, Mitch McConnell, Lisa Murkowski and Rand Paul, voting in favour.[[S024 Vote 160]] The tariffs on Canada nonetheless remained.

Through the summer the two neighbours' paths diverged further. On 31 July the United States raised the duty on Canadian goods outside the agreement from 25 to 35 per cent from 1 August, citing Canada's retaliation.[[S016 Sec. 1-2]] The same day President Sheinbaum announced a ninety-day standstill for Mexico after a call with President Trump: no increase, the agreement's goods still exempt, and the existing tariffs on cars, steel and aluminium left in place.[[S029 Communiqué, 31 July 2025]] A month later Canada retreated. From 1 September it removed most of its March counter-tariffs, keeping those on steel, aluminium and cars, on the ground that most Canadian goods now entered the United States free under the agreement.[[S026 Archived response page]] By the end of the year the neighbour that had never retaliated and the one that had stood in much the same place.

## The April shock

The 2 April order was followed by days of turbulence in financial markets. Federal Reserve staff later described the prices of risky assets as "extremely volatile" amid tariff developments, and the tariff announcements as causing "a significant deterioration in global risk sentiment."[[S052 Staff review of the financial situation]] Equity prices fell sharply before recovering.[[S052 Developments in financial markets]]

The bond market moved too. The yield on ten-year Treasury notes fell from 4.20 per cent on 2 April to 4.01 per cent on 4 April and then rose to 4.48 per cent by 11 April, a rise of 47 basis points in a week.[[S051 Rows 31 March-16 April 2025]] Fed staff noted that liquidity in the Treasury market deteriorated after 2 April, though the market continued to function, and that the trade-weighted dollar fell by more than 2 per cent.[[S052 Staff review of the financial situation]]

China retaliated first. On 4 April its State Council Tariff Commission announced a 34 per cent tariff on all American goods, effective 10 April, and the commerce ministry imposed export licensing on seven rare earths.[[S006 Sec. 1 | S038 Announcement No. 18 of 2025]] On 8 April the United States raised its rate on China from 34 to 84 per cent, effective the next day.[[S006 Sec. 2]] China matched it the following day.[[S035 Announcement of 9 April 2025]]

Then, on 9 April, the President changed course for everyone but China. A new order suspended the country-specific rates for ninety days, until 9 July, leaving a flat 10 per cent, and raised the rate on China again, to 125 per cent.[[S007 Sec. 2-3]] The order gave its reason: "more than 75 other foreign trading partners" had approached the United States to negotiate rather than retaliate.[[S007 Preamble, 90 FR 15626]] Two days later a presidential memorandum exempted computers, smartphones and semiconductors from the reciprocal tariffs altogether, retroactive to 5 April, with refunds for duties already paid.[[S050 Memorandum, 11 April 2025]] Fed staff recorded that the deterioration in sentiment "largely reversed" after the pause.[[S052 Staff review of the financial situation]]

## The China spiral and the truce

With China, escalation had begun in February and was answered step for step. The 10 per cent duty of 4 February brought Chinese duties from 10 February of 15 per cent on American coal and liquefied natural gas and 10 per cent on crude oil, farm machinery, large cars and pickups.[[S005 Sec. 2(a) | S033 Announcement No. 1 of 2025]] When the American duty rose to 20 per cent in March, China imposed from 10 March duties of 15 per cent on chicken, wheat, corn and cotton and 10 per cent on sorghum, soybeans, pork, beef, seafood, fruit, vegetables and dairy.[[S010 Sec. 2 | S034 Announcement No. 2 of 2025]]

In April the two rates chased each other upward. After the American increase to 125 per cent, China raised its own to 125 per cent from 12 April, adding that further American increases would make no difference: "Even if the United States continues to raise tariffs, such action would serve no economic purpose."[[S036 Announcement of 11 April 2025]] On most Chinese goods the United States now charged 125 per cent under the April order plus 20 per cent under the fentanyl order, a total of 145 per cent.[[S007 Sec. 3 | S010 Sec. 2]] Duties on low-value parcels from China, which lost their exemption from 2 May, were raised to 120 per cent of value.[[S006 Sec. 3 | S007 Sec. 3]]

{{figure:china-rates}}

Within a month both sides stepped back. After talks in Geneva, a joint statement of 12 May committed each to suspend 24 percentage points of its tariff for ninety days, keep 10 per cent, and cancel the April increases, by 14 May.[[S008 US and China commitments]] China also undertook to suspend or remove the non-tariff countermeasures it had taken since 2 April, and talks were to continue between Vice Premier He Lifeng on one side and the Treasury Secretary, Scott Bessent, and the Trade Representative, Jamieson Greer, on the other.[[S008 China commitments; mechanism]] China duly cut its rate to 10 per cent from 14 May.[[S037 Announcement of 13 May 2025]] With the fentanyl duty unchanged, the American rate on most Chinese goods fell to 30 per cent.[[S008 Derived | S010 Sec. 2]]

{{portrait:xi}}

China's strongest lever, however, was not its tariff. On 9 October it announced far broader controls on rare earths, including on foreign-made products containing Chinese material and on rare-earth technologies.[[S009 China commitments | S039 Announcement No. 70 of 2025]] At the end of October the two governments reached a wider arrangement in the Republic of Korea. By the American account, China agreed to suspend the October controls, to grant general licences for exports of rare earths, gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite to American users, to suspend its retaliatory tariffs and countermeasures, and to buy at least 12 million tonnes of American soybeans in the last two months of 2025 and 25 million tonnes a year from 2026 to 2028.[[S009 China commitments]] The United States agreed to cut the fentanyl duty by 10 percentage points from 10 November and to keep the higher reciprocal rate suspended until 10 November 2026.[[S009 US commitments | S011 Sec. 1-2]] China's commerce ministry suspended the October controls until the same date, though the April licensing of seven rare earths remained.[[S039 Announcement No. 70 of 2025]] The year ended with the American duty on most Chinese goods at 20 per cent, double what it had been in February and a seventh of its April peak.[[S008 Derived | S011 Sec. 1-2]]

## Deals and grievances

For other partners the ninety-day pause became a period of negotiation. It ended with a new order on 31 July that set country rates from 7 August.[[S015 Sec. 2]] The new schedule rewarded and punished. Switzerland's rate rose to 39 per cent and Laos and Myanmar's to 40; Taiwan and Vietnam were set at 20, much of Southeast Asia at 19, Japan and South Korea at 15, and the United Kingdom and Brazil at 10.[[S015 Annex I, 90 FR 37967-37969]] Goods found to have been shipped through third countries to evade the duties faced 40 per cent.[[S015 Sec. 3]]

The largest partners bought their rates with commitments. Under a framework published on 21 August, the European Union accepted a ceiling of 15 per cent on most of its goods, or the ordinary rate where that was higher, and said it intended to remove its tariffs on all American industrial goods, buy $750 billion of American energy through 2028 and at least $40 billion of AI chips, and expected European companies to invest a further $600 billion in the United States.[[S046 Items 1-6 | S015 Sec. 2(c)]] Japan's agreement, put into effect on 4 September and backdated to 7 August, set the same 15 per cent ceiling and recorded a Japanese undertaking to invest $550 billion in projects selected by the American government.[[S047 Preamble and Sec. 2]] South Korea's, confirmed in a joint fact sheet on 13 November after the two presidents met in Gyeongju on 29 October, set 15 per cent on Korean goods, including cars, against $150 billion of investment in shipbuilding and $200 billion more under a memorandum, capped at $20 billion a year.[[S048 Trade and investment sections | S049 Summary and dates]] These were commitments, not completed investments, and whether they will be fulfilled lies beyond this chapter.

Two partners were punished for reasons that had little to do with trade. On 30 July an order imposed an additional 40 per cent on Brazilian goods from 6 August, citing Brazilian court orders that compelled American platforms to censor content, the conduct of Justice Alexandre de Moraes, and the prosecution of former President Jair Bolsonaro, which the order called persecution.[[S031 Sec. 1-2]] Aircraft, energy, fertilizers and some metals were exempted.[[S031 Sec. 2(b)]] In November, after negotiations had begun, certain Brazilian farm products were relieved of the duty.[[S032 Preamble and Sec. 1]] On 6 August another order added 25 per cent on Indian goods from 27 August because India was importing Russian oil, which with India's reciprocal rate of 25 per cent made 50.[[S018 Sec. 2 | S015 Annex I]]

Meanwhile the national-security tariffs widened. Steel and aluminium were raised from 25 to 50 per cent from 4 June.[[S041 Clause 1]] Semi-finished copper products were taxed at 50 per cent from 1 August, softwood timber at 10 per cent and upholstered furniture, kitchen cabinets and vanities at 25 per cent from 14 October, and medium and heavy trucks at 25 per cent and buses at 10 from 1 November.[[S042 Clause 2 | S043 Clauses 1-3, 7 | S045 Clause 1]] The timber proclamation honoured the new agreements, capping its rate at 10 per cent for Britain and at 15 per cent in total for the European Union and Japan.[[S043 Clause 7]] And from 29 August the duty-free treatment of small parcels ended for every country, replaced for postal shipments by flat duties of $80, $160 or $200 an item.[[S017 Sec. 2-4]]

## Courts and Congress

The emergency tariffs were challenged almost at once, and the challengers won. On 28 May a three-judge panel of the Court of International Trade granted summary judgment to five small businesses and twelve States.[[S012 Opinion, pp. 1-2 | S003 Syllabus, p. 2]] It held that the emergency statute authorized none of the orders: the worldwide and retaliatory tariffs exceeded any power to regulate importation, and the drug-trafficking tariffs failed because they did not deal with the threats they named. "IEEPA does not authorize any of the Worldwide, Retaliatory, or Trafficking Tariff Orders," the court concluded, and it vacated them for everyone.[[S012 Conclusion, pp. 48-49]]

The ruling changed nothing at the border. On 10 June the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit stayed it pending appeal and ordered the case heard by all its active judges.[[S014 Order, pp. 3-4]] On 29 August that court affirmed, by seven votes to four, that the trafficking and reciprocal tariffs exceeded the statute, describing them as "unbounded in scope, amount, and duration"; but it sent back the question of how broad the remedy should be.[[S013 Per curiam; Part VI | S003 Syllabus, p. 2]] The tariffs stayed in force while the government took the case to the Supreme Court, and they were collected throughout the year.

In Congress, the Senate voted on 2 April to end the Canadian emergency; the tariffs on Canada nonetheless remained in force.[[S024 Vote 160]]

## The world trades around America

The rest of the world did not stop trading; it traded around the United States. China's exports to the United States fell by 19.5 per cent in 2025, while its exports to Southeast Asia rose by 14.0 per cent and to the European Union by 9.0 per cent.[[S053 Table 13]] Its trade surplus reached $1,188.98 billion.[[S054 Table, January-December 2025]] American figures show the same movement from the other side: the goods deficit with China fell by $93.4 billion, while the deficits with Taiwan and Vietnam rose by $73.0 billion and $54.7 billion.[[S019 Annual Summary for 2025]]

{{figure:deficit-shift}}

Other governments signed agreements without the United States. Britain and India signed a trade agreement on 24 July.[[S058 Collection page]] The four members of the European Free Trade Association and the four of Mercosur signed one in Rio de Janeiro on 16 September.[[S055 Joint communiqué]] The European Union and Indonesia concluded negotiations a week later, on 23 September.[[S056 Agreements page]] China and the members of ASEAN signed an upgrade of their free trade area in Kuala Lumpur on 28 October.[[S057 News item, 29 October 2025]] Even Mexico, which had refused to retaliate against the United States, closed the year by raising its own tariffs, mostly to between 25 and 35 per cent and to 50 per cent on cars, on imports from countries with which it had no trade agreement, from 1 January 2026.[[S030 Decree and transitional articles]]

## What the tariffs did

The tariffs raised a great deal of money. Net customs receipts, about $7 billion to $8 billion a month at the start of the year, reached $15.6 billion in April and about $30 billion a month from August; for the calendar year they came to about $264.0 billion, against $78.9 billion in 2024.[[S021 Customs Duties, monthly net receipts]] One independent estimate put the average effective tariff rate faced by American consumers at 16.8 per cent in November, the highest since 1935.[[S022 Key Takeaways 2]]

{{figure:customs}}

They did not shrink the deficit the emergency was declared to cure. In the first full-year estimate, the goods deficit rose by $25.5 billion, or 2.1 per cent, to $1,240.9 billion.[[S019 Annual Summary for 2025]] The broader deficit in goods and services fell by $2.1 billion, to $901.5 billion, only because the surplus in services grew.[[S019 Annual Summary for 2025]] A later revision raised the goods deficit further, by about 1.5 per cent.[[S020 Notice, p. 1]] Supporters of the policy could point to the sharp fall in the deficit with China; critics, to the rises with Taiwan and Vietnam.

Their effect on prices remains disputed. In April 2025 the Budget Lab at Yale had estimated that the tariffs would raise consumer prices by 2.3 per cent; a year later it reported that retrospective studies put the effect at between 0.5 and 1.0 per cent.[[S023 Key Takeaways 1]] It suggested that part of the gap reflected rates that changed or were never put into effect, and that part of the cost might not yet have reached consumers or might have been borne as lower incomes rather than higher prices.[[S023 Key Takeaways 4-5]] The question is not settled.

The legal question was. On 20 February 2026, after the year this chapter covers, the Supreme Court held by six votes to three that "IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs."[[S003 Syllabus, pp. 1-2]] The Chief Justice wrote for the Court, joined in its central parts by Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Barrett and Jackson; Justices Thomas, Alito and Kavanaugh dissented.[[S003 Syllabus, p. 6]] The ruling reached the drug-trafficking tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China and the reciprocal tariffs of April alike.[[S003 Syllabus, pp. 1-2]] It did not concern the national-security tariffs on steel, aluminium, copper, cars, trucks and timber, which rested on another statute.[[S003 Syllabus, pp. 1-2 | S040 Clauses 1-2]] The year's most sweeping trade policy had been built on a power the President did not have, and for most of 2025 nothing stopped it from operating.
