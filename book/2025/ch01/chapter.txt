## The first day

On 20 January 2025 President Donald Trump signed a set of orders that announced how he meant to govern.[[S061 Heading | S062 Heading | S063 Heading]] One created a "Department of Government Efficiency," not a department at all but a temporary organization inside the renamed United States DOGE Service, due to expire on 4 July 2026.[[S061 Sec. 3]] Every agency was told to set up its own DOGE team of at least four employees within thirty days.[[S061 Sec. 3(c)]]

{{portrait:trump}}

A second order addressed citizenship. It directed federal agencies to stop recognizing as citizens children born in the United States to mothers who were present unlawfully or only temporarily, unless the father was a citizen or a lawful permanent resident, for births more than thirty days after the order.[[S062 Sec. 1-2]] A third act, a proclamation, reached back to the attack on the Capitol on 6 January 2021: it commuted the sentences of fourteen named people and granted "a full, complete and unconditional pardon" to everyone else convicted of offences connected with that day.[[S063 Proclamation]]

These three instruments set the pattern for the year. Each was an exercise of power by the President alone. Each tested a different limit: the reach of executive reorganization, the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment, and the pardon power. And each would be contested, in the courts or in public, for months. This chapter follows that contest. It argues that the presidency moved fast and far in 2025, and that the institution that checked it was, above all, the federal judiciary, often late and sometimes in the President's favour.

## Cutting the state

The order itself spoke of modernizing federal technology and software.[[S061 Sec. 4]] The change in the federal government that can be measured for the year is in its workforce. Federal employment, which stood at 3,010,000 in January, fell in every month of the year that followed and ended it at 2,722,000, a loss of 288,000 jobs.[[S077 Monthly values, 2024-2025]]

{{figure:federal-jobs}}

The largest single fall came late. In the current series, federal employment fell from 2,914,000 in September to 2,748,000 in October.[[S077 Monthly values, 2024-2025]] In its first estimate, published in December, the Bureau of Labor Statistics put the October drop at 162,000 and attributed it to employees who had accepted a "deferred resignation" offer leaving the payroll.[[S078 Establishment survey commentary]] By November, it reported, federal employment was down by 271,000 from its January peak.[[S078 Establishment survey commentary]] Federal employment ended 2025 nearly a tenth lower than it began.[[S077 Derived]]

## Deportation and the Alien Enemies Act

On 14 March the President invoked the Alien Enemies Act against the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua, which his proclamation described as invading the United States.[[S064 Heading; Sec. 1]] It declared that Venezuelan citizens aged fourteen or older who were members of the gang, and were not naturalized citizens or lawful permanent residents, were liable to be apprehended, restrained and removed as "alien enemies."[[S064 Sec. 1, 3]]

The Supreme Court intervened twice within a week. On 7 April it ruled that men held under the proclamation had to bring their challenges as petitions for habeas corpus in the district where they were confined, which for these detainees meant Texas rather than the court in Washington that had first blocked their removal.[[S065 Per curiam]] But it also held that they were "entitled to notice and opportunity to be heard" before removal, so that they could seek habeas relief in time.[[S065 Per curiam]]

Three days later the Court turned to a single case. On 15 March the government had removed Kilmar Abrego Garcia to detention in El Salvador.[[S066 Order]] It acknowledged that an earlier order forbade his removal there, so that the removal was illegal, and attributed it to an "administrative error."[[S066 Order]] A district court in Maryland ordered the government to bring him back. The Supreme Court left the core of that order in place: the government was to "facilitate" his release and ensure that his case was handled as it would have been had he not been improperly sent there.[[S066 Order and statement]] The two rulings framed the year's argument over deportation. The courts allowed the government to pursue its aims, but they insisted on procedure, and the dispute over what "facilitate" required became a measure of how far the administration would go in honouring a court's demands.

## Soldiers in American cities

On 7 June, as protests against federal immigration officers grew in Los Angeles, the President called at least 2,000 members of the National Guard into federal service for sixty days, to protect Immigration and Customs Enforcement and other federal personnel and property.[[S068 Memorandum | S069 Background]] He relied on section 12406 of title 10 of the United States Code, and the number federalized reached 4,000.[[S068 Memorandum | S069 Summary]]

California sued. Judge Charles Breyer found that none of the conditions the statute requires for federalization existed and that the order had not been issued through the Governor, and he ordered the Guard returned to the Governor's control.[[S069 Summary]] On 19 June the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit stayed his order, holding that the President had probably acted within the statute.[[S069 Summary and order]] In August the President turned to the capital. An order of 11 August declared a "crime emergency" in the District of Columbia and, under the Home Rule Act, directed the city to place its Metropolitan Police Department at the service of the federal government.[[S070 Sec. 1-2]]

The courts drew a firmer line in Illinois. On 4 October the President federalized 300 members of the Illinois National Guard to protect federal officers in and around Chicago, and on the next day Guard members from Texas were federalized and sent there too.[[S071 Per curiam, pp. 1-2]] A district court barred their deployment, and the Court of Appeals kept the bar in place.[[S071 Per curiam, pp. 1-2]] On 23 December the Supreme Court refused to lift it. The statute let the President call out the Guard if he was "unable with the regular forces to execute the laws," and the Court concluded that "regular forces" likely meant the regular military.[[S071 Per curiam, pp. 2-3]] Because the Posse Comitatus Act generally forbids the military to execute the laws, the President would first need some authority to use the army for that purpose, and the government had identified none.[[S071 Per curiam, pp. 2-3]] Justices Alito, Thomas and Gorsuch dissented.[[S071 Order and opinions]] After months of deployments, the Supreme Court ended the year by holding that, on this statute at least, the President probably could not send the Guard where he had sent it.

## Courts and Congress

The courts were the main check on the President in 2025, but the Supreme Court also limited their reach. Opponents of the citizenship order brought three suits, and federal district courts responded with injunctions barring its enforcement against anyone, anywhere.[[S067 Opinion, Part I]] On 27 June, in Trump v. CASA, the Supreme Court held by six votes to three that such "universal injunctions likely exceed the equitable authority that Congress has given to federal courts."[[S067 Syllabus]] Justice Barrett wrote for the Chief Justice and Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch and Kavanaugh; Justice Sotomayor, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson, dissented, as did Justice Jackson separately.[[S067 Syllabus]] The Court did not decide whether the citizenship order was lawful; it decided how far a single judge's order could reach.[[S067 Syllabus]]

Congress passed a budget reconciliation act, Public Law 119-21, signed on 4 July.[[S072 Heading]] It did not pass the annual budget on time. No regular appropriations had been enacted by the start of the fiscal year on 1 October, and the government shut down.[[S074 Funding gap history]] The shutdown ended only on 12 November, when the President signed a law that funded three groups of agencies for the full year and the rest of the government until 30 January 2026.[[S073 Heading; Sec. 106 | S074 Funding gap history]] At 42 full days it was the longest funding gap since at least 1977, eight days longer than the previous record set in 2018-2019.[[S074 Funding gap history]]

On one subject the House acted almost unanimously. It voted 427 to 1 on 18 November to require the Justice Department to publish its records on Jeffrey Epstein.[[S076 Vote totals]] The President signed the bill the next day; it gave the Attorney General thirty days to make the records public.[[S075 Sec. 2]]

## What checked the President

The year's record suggests that the second Trump presidency used executive power with a speed and breadth that the courts could only partly follow. Orders on government, citizenship and pardons were signed on the first day.[[S061 Heading | S062 Heading | S063 Heading]] The Alien Enemies Act was used against a criminal gang, and the National Guard was federalized in California and Illinois.[[S064 Sec. 1 | S068 Memorandum | S071 Per curiam, pp. 1-2]] Federal employment fell by nearly 300,000.[[S077 Monthly values, 2024-2025]]

The checks came mostly from judges. They insisted on notice before deportation, required the government to facilitate the return of a man it had deported illegally, and in December held that the Guard could not be sent to Chicago on the President's chosen authority.[[S065 Per curiam | S066 Order and statement | S071 Per curiam, pp. 2-3]] Yet the Supreme Court also curtailed nationwide injunctions, and an appeals court allowed the Guard to stay federalized in California while the case went on.[[S067 Syllabus | S069 Summary and order]] Congress, for its part, let the government shut down for six weeks and ordered the Epstein files opened.[[S074 Funding gap history | S075 Sec. 2]] The limits on the presidency in 2025 were real, but they were set case by case, and usually after the fact.
